Designing credit for
a world that
doesn’t stabilise

Credit tends to freeze when conditions change. Today, that simply isn’t good enough. In a world that doesn’t stabilise, credit must adapt in real time, staying available when consumers and economies need it most.

Credit still assumes stability

When a crisis hits, one of the first casualties is access to credit. Lenders thrive on predictability to underpin their decisions on whether to advance funds. When that predictability vanishes, whether thanks to market volatility, trade disruption or war, lenders become defensive and the availability of credit shrinks.

The problem here is that credit often contracts just when people need financial flexibility most. And traditional credit models don’t always account for the way people earn today.

For example, a consumer with a traditional nine-to-five job and a long-term mortgage might weather the storm, ticking all the right boxes for legacy credit agencies. But those in the gig economy, with variable income patterns and gaps in their employment history, tend to be left behind at the worst possible time.

If you are somebody who is intrinsically in a more uncertain situation, like a gig economy worker, you’re probably going to be impacted more by crisis.

Rob Macmillan PayCredit Group Product Manager Paymentology

The answer lies in technology that gives lenders a real-time view of a customer’s financial situation, enabling a more dynamic approach than traditional credit models provide. “The future of credit needs to be responsive, because it cannot keep relying on stale snapshots of the user,” says Nida Khan, Product Manager at Paymentology.

Credit decisions must be faster and informed by context, including temporary economic shocks and changes in how people earn. Someone might move from salaried employment to freelancing and back again as work opportunities evolve.

Too many people are still excluded simply because their financial lives do not fit the traditional model.

Nida Khan Product Manager Paymentology

A credit system that pays greater attention to people’s circumstances will support consumers through volatility rather than abandoning them.

From fixed credit to adaptive
credit

Building a credit system for an unpredictable world also requires rethinking how credit works once it has been provided.

Consider a consumer who is suddenly hit by an unexpected, but one-off cost, such as a thousand-dollar medical or car repair bill. The expense could put them under temporary financial pressure, even if they have otherwise managed their credit well. With a rigid legacy system, the borrower may simply be left to default, and the system punishes them if they do.

Technology can give lenders a more nuanced understanding of the customer, helping them recognise an unexpected expense as an unavoidable one-off rather than a failure to plan or act responsibly.

Such a system will ideally not only recognise the true reason for the financial stress, but be constructive about a solution, perhaps converting that specific transaction from a revolving credit balance into a more manageable instalment plan over several months.

Rich data and artificial intelligence can help providers move beyond blunt risk assessments, benefiting both lenders and borrowers. “Our focus is enabling our clients to give customers more informed decisions,” says Macmillan. Intelligent credit systems can empower customers to adjust their repayment schedules as their circumstances change. Lending like this is responsible and sustainable, but also more flexible and intelligent.

Global scale, local control

A more adaptive global credit apparatus requires navigating a complex and fragmented regulatory environment. Credit is increasingly global, but compliance is local, market by market.

Even within unified blocs like Europe, regulatory directives around credit are applied differently. New products can reshape the regulatory landscape. The emergence of buy now, pay later (BNPL), for example, started out operating under the regulatory radar but is now subject to increasingly strict oversight in many jurisdictions. Other markets are seeing the introduction of caps on annual percentage rates (APRs).

So, any platform seeking to bridge modern credit gaps must carry off the difficult double act of scaling globally while complying with a wide variety of local rules. From a technology perspective, this requires cloud-based systems that can be configured to a changing landscape. A leading platform must allow risk and compliance teams to dynamically adjust risk repayment hierarchies as regulations evolve, without waiting months for a software update.

The infrastructure behind

Credit that doesn’t stand still

The future of credit must be responsive, inclusive and accountable, with hyper-personalisation fuelled by AI and large language models. Consumers will expect their credit providers to become more than a source of lending, acting as intelligent partners who can help them manage their financial lives.

But using AI in credit also comes with risk. Its operational efficiencies must not come at the cost of transparency or allow credit decisioning to disappear into an unaccountable black box. The ideal solution combines technological lift with human consideration to ensure credit serves the historically excluded without increasing risk to the lender.

Get the balance right, and credit will no longer freeze in the face of volatility. Instead, a more resilient system can emerge, giving consumers the financial flexibility they need to thrive in an unpredictable world.
