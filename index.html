Designing credit for
a world that
doesn’t stabilise

Credit tends to freeze when conditions change, and that’s no longer good enough. In a world that doesn’t stabilise, credit must adapt in real time, staying available when consumers and economies need it most.

Credit still assumes stability

When a crisis hits, one of the first casualties is access to credit. Lenders thrive on predictability to underpin their decisions on whether to advance funds. When that predictability vanishes, whether thanks to market volatility, trade disruption or war, lenders become defensive and the availability of credit shrinks.

It is a fundamental problem that this contraction happens at the time consumers need financial flexibility the most. Established credit models have failed to keep pace with the evolution of how people work. A consumer with a traditional nine-to-five job and a long-term mortgage might weather the storm, ticking all the right boxes for legacy credit agencies. But those in the gig economy, with variable income patterns and gaps in their employment history, tend to be left behind at the worst possible time.

“If you are somebody who is intrinsically in a more uncertain situation, like a gig economy worker, you’re probably going to be impacted more by crisis,” says Rob Macmillan, PayCredit Group Product Manager at Paymentology.

The solution to this problem lies in harnessing technology, and the real-time transparency it offers, to create a more dynamic environment for the evaluation of credit than today’s inflexible models provide. “The future of credit needs to be responsive, because it cannot keep relying on stale snapshots of the user,” says Nida Khan, Product Manager at Paymentology.

Credit decisions must be faster and informed by context, such as temporary economic shocks and the changing nature of how people earn, sometimes pivoting from salaried employment to freelancing and back again as their work opportunities change.

Too many people are still excluded simply because their financial lives do not fit the traditional model.

Nida Khan Product Manager

An environment paying greater attention to this context will support consumers through volatility rather than abandoning them.

The issue isn’t that crises happen. It’s that credit systems are still designed for stability.

From fixed credit to adaptive
credit

This is not just a question of initial credit decisions, but bringing greater dynamism once credit is already advanced.

Consider a consumer who is suddenly hit by an unexpected, but one-off cost: a thousand-dollar medical or car repair bill, for example. A rigid legacy system waits to see if the borrower defaults and punishes them if they do. A system using technology to gain a more nuanced understanding of the customer will recognise this for the anomaly it is: something that doesn’t represent a customer failure to plan or to act with prudence, but one of those unavoidable challenges that arise in life from time to time.

Such a system will ideally not only recognise the true reason for the financial stress, but be constructive about a solution, perhaps converting that specific transaction from a revolving credit balance into a more manageable instalment plan over several months.

Rich data and artificial intelligence can help providers move beyond blunt risk assessments to the benefit of themselves and their borrowers. “Our focus is enabling our clients to give customers more informed decisions,” says Macmillan. Intelligent credit systems can empower customers to adjust their repayment schedules as their circumstances change. Lending like this is responsible and sustainable, but also more flexible and intelligent.

Global scale, local control

Changing the world’s credit apparatus requires navigating a complex and fragmented regulatory environment. Credit is increasingly global, but compliance is local, market by market.

Even within unified blocs like Europe, there is variation in the way that regulatory directives around credit are applied. New products or themes change the regulatory landscape afresh: the emergence of buy now, pay later (BNPL), for example, started out operating under the regulatory radar but is now subject to increasingly strict oversight in many jurisdictions. Other markets are seeing the introduction of caps on annual percentage rates (APRs).

So, any platform seeking to bridge modern credit gaps must carry off a difficult double act: scaling globally while complying with a wide variety of local rules. From a technology perspective, this requires cloud-based systems that can be configured to a changing landscape. A leading platform must allow risk and compliance teams to dynamically adjust risk repayment hierarchies in line with changing regulation, without waiting months for a software update.

Credit that doesn’t stand still

The future of credit must be responsive, inclusive and accountable, with hyper-personalisation fuelled by AI and large language models. Consumers will expect their credit providers not just to be a source of lending but intelligent partners who can help them manage their financial lifestyles.

This comes with risk: the operational efficiencies that AI brings must not come at the cost of transparency, with credit decisioning disappearing into an unaccountable black box. The ideal solution is a combination of technological lift and human consideration in ensuring that credit serves the historically excluded without increasing risk to the lender.

With that achieved, credit will no longer freeze in the face of volatility. Instead, a new system will emerge that is resilient to shocks and allows the vital flow of credit to consumers to thrive in an unpredictable world.
